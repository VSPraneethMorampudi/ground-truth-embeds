    One base, many departments

    What we do: we build one trusted map of Chhattisgarh. We collect spatial data from every department, check it, fit it to one base, and publish it for everyone, so a village is the same in every layer. How it works, in the sections below: data layers on one shared base; an API that serves them as live data services; the Geo Portal to view, search and download; use cases for planning and decisions; and a mobile app for viewing and editing in the field, whose updates feed back into the data layers. Spatial data reaches the State Geoportal from departments across Chhattisgarh. The Revenue Department supplies the base: district, tehsil, block and village boundaries for the whole state. Every other department's data arrives on top of that base, each source in its own format and to its own schedule. Mining, Anganwadi and BharatNet are three of them. The work is to merge those submissions onto one set of boundaries and one coordinate system, so that a village means the same village in every layer. Today that merge holds 162 layers from 42 departments, published to one standard. Three applications already run on it: Mining, Anganwadi and BharatNet.

    Old records, made GIS-ready

    Old maps on paper or scanned, Excel sheets with latitude and longitude, KML files from Google Earth, CAD drawings and older shapefiles are each converted: georeferenced, their geometry fixed, and put into one coordinate system. Each becomes a layer on the same map of Chhattisgarh: district boundaries, Anganwadi centres, fibre routes, mine leases and highways.

    Three ways to map what's new

    New map data is created in three ways. From satellite images, roads and buildings are traced on screen. From old paper maps, the map is scanned and pinned to real coordinates with control points, then traced. In the field, places are measured on the ground: a phone GPS to within metres, DGPS to centimetres, a total station to millimetres, and a drone covers a whole area quickly. All three feed the same map layer.

    One central copy, always current

    Before, each department kept its own copy of the map, at different versions. Now the data is centralised: every department sends its updates to one central GIS database, and the change reaches every department at once, so everyone sees the same version.
